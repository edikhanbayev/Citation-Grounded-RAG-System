Question: What are the standard teaching hours on Wednesday and Friday during the UK University teaching week?
================================================================================

[1/2] Running Plain LLM...

[PLAIN LLM ANSWER]
There isn’t a single, UK‑wide timetable that dictates exactly what hours are taught on any particular day of a “teaching week.”  Each university (and often each faculty or department within a university) sets its own contact‑hour schedule, so the start‑ and finish‑times on Wednesday and Friday can differ quite a lot from one institution to another.

**What you’ll usually see in practice**

| Typical pattern | Why it looks that way | Rough time‑range you might encounter |
|-----------------|----------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **Monday‑Friday, 9 am‑5 pm** (with a 1‑hour lunch break) | Mirrors a conventional office day and makes it easy to slot lectures, seminars and labs into a regular block. | Lectures often run 9‑11 am, 11‑1 pm, 2‑4 pm; a Friday afternoon slot may be used for labs, tutorials or assessment. |
| **Staggered start times** (e.g., 8:30 am‑4:30 pm or 10 am‑6 pm) | Allows departments to fit more sessions into a day or to accommodate part‑time staff and students. | Same total contact hours, just shifted earlier or later. |
| **Reduced Friday schedule** (e.g., no teaching after 3 pm) | Many institutions reserve late‑Friday for seminars, workshops, field trips, or administrative duties. | Lectures may finish by 2‑3 pm, with optional labs or tutorials later. |
| **Block teaching** (e.g., two‑hour blocks with a longer lunch) | Helps avoid “lecture fatigue” and gives students time for independent study. | 9‑11 am, 12‑2 pm, 3‑5 pm blocks are common. |
| **Hybrid/online days** | Some universities blend face‑to‑face and virtual sessions, especially after the pandemic. | A Wednesday might have a mix of in‑person and online tutorials, with the same overall contact‑hour total. |

**Typical weekly contact‑hour totals**

- **Undergraduate programmes** often aim for **12–15 contact hours per week** (spread over 2–4 days).  
- **Post‑graduate taught programmes** may have **6–12 contact hours per week**, sometimes concentrated on fewer days.  

If a programme spreads those hours evenly, you might see roughly **2–3 contact hours on each weekday**, meaning a Wednesday could have two 2‑hour lectures (e.g., 10 am‑12 pm) and a Friday could have a single 3‑hour lab (e.g., 1‑4 pm). But many courses cluster sessions (e.g., two back‑to‑back lectures on Monday and a longer workshop on Thursday), so the pattern can be irregular.

**What to do if you need the exact schedule**

1. **Check the university’s academic calendar** – it will list the start/end dates of teaching weeks, holidays, and any “no‑teaching” days.  
2. **Look at the department’s timetable** – most institutions publish a weekly timetable (often via a student portal such as Moodle, Blackboard, or a dedicated timetabling system).  
3. **Ask the programme coordinator or student services** – they can confirm whether there are any university‑wide conventions (e.g., “no teaching after 4 pm on Fridays”).  

**Bottom line**

- There is **no UK‑wide statutory standard** for teaching hours on Wednesday or Friday.  
- Most universities operate within a **9 am‑5 pm window**, with variations for early‑ or late‑starting days, reduced Friday afternoons, or block‑style scheduling.  
- The exact hours you’ll encounter depend on the **specific university, faculty, and programme** you’re looking at. If you need the precise schedule for a particular institution, the best source is that university’s own timetabling portal or the relevant academic office.

## 2. Integrated Master of Science (MSci)

ID: 22
Question: How is the overall degree mark calculated across study years for a student undertaking a Study Abroad program?
================================================================================

[1/2] Running Plain LLM...

[PLAIN LLM ANSWER]
**In most universities the “overall degree mark” (or final classification) is a weighted average of the marks you earn in every module you complete during the whole programme.**  
When you spend a semester or a year abroad the same principle applies – the modules you take overseas are simply added to the pool of credit‑bearing modules, but there are a few extra steps you need to be aware of because the grading scale used by the host institution is often different from the one used at home.

Below is a generic, step‑by‑step outline of how the calculation is usually done.  The exact numbers (credit values, weighting percentages, conversion tables, etc.) will differ from one university to another, so you should always check your own institution’s handbook or the Study‑Abroad office for the precise rules that apply to you.

---

## 1.  Every module has a **credit value** (or credit points)

| Year | Typical credit load | Example credit values |
|------|--------------------|-----------------------|
| Year 1 | 120 credits (full‑time) | 20 credits per module (6 modules) |
| Year 2 | 120 credits | same as Year 1 |
| Year 3 (or 4) | 120 credits | same as Year 1 |
| Study‑abroad semester | 60 credits (or whatever the host university awards) | often 30 credits per semester |

*The total number of credits you need to graduate is usually fixed (e.g., 360 credits for a three‑year programme).*

---

## 2.  Convert **overseas grades** to the home‑university scale

1. **Obtain the host‑university grading rubric** (e.g., A‑B‑C‑D‑E, 1‑5, percentages, etc.).  
2. **Use the conversion table** supplied by your home institution.  
   * Example (UK → UK conversion):  
     * 70 % + → First‑class (70)  
     * 60‑69 % → Upper Second (2:1) (60)  
     * 50‑59 % → Lower Second (2:2) (50)  
     * 40‑49 % → Third (40)  
   * Example (US GPA → UK %):  
     * A = 75 %  
     * B = 65 %  
     * C = 55 %  
     * D = 45 %  
   * Some universities simply **record the overseas module as “Pass/Fail”**; in that case it contributes the credit value but does not affect the numeric average (it only satisfies the credit requirement).

3. **Round or adjust** according to any specific rules (e.g., “round up to the nearest whole number” or “apply a 5‑point bonus for accredited study‑abroad modules”).

---

## 3.  Compute a **weighted average for each academic year**

For each year you do:

\[
\text{Year‑average} = \frac{\sum (\text{module mark} \times \text{module credits})}{\sum (\text{module credits})}
\]

*All modules taken that year—including the abroad ones—are included in the numerator and denominator.*

**Example (Year 2 with a semester abroad):**

| Module | Credits | Mark (home scale) |
|--------|---------|-------------------|
| Accounting | 20 | 68 |
| Marketing | 20 | 72 |
| Study‑abroad: International Business | 30 | 65 (converted) |
| Study‑abroad: Language | 10 | 70 (converted) |
| **Total** | **80** | — |

\[
\text{Year‑average}= \frac{(68\times20)+(72\times20)+(65\times30)+(70\times10)}{80}= \frac{1360+1440+1950+700}{80}= \frac{5450}{80}=68.1
\]

---

## 4.  Combine the **year‑averages** into the **overall degree mark**

Most institutions weight the later years more heavily because they contain the “core” or “advanced” modules. A common weighting scheme is:

| Year | Weight (percentage of final mark) |
|------|-----------------------------------|
| Year 1 | 0 % (often excluded from final classification) |
| Year 2 | 30 % |
| Year 3 | 40 % |
| Year 4 (if applicable) | 30 % |

If your programme is three years, the weights might be 30 % + 30 % + 40 % (or 33 % each).  The study‑abroad semester is already baked into the year‑average, so you do **not** apply any extra weight to it.

The overall mark is then:

\[
\text{Overall mark}= \sum (\text{Year‑average} \times \text{Year‑weight})
\]

**Continuing the example (three‑year programme, Year 1 excluded):**

| Year | Year‑average | Weight | Contribution |
|------|--------------|--------|--------------|

**What “cohort legislation” means**

In many higher‑education systems the law (or a set of regulations) is written so that it applies to a *cohort*—the group of students who first enrol in a particular academic year.  The idea is to give students a stable set of rules for the duration of their programme, even if the government later changes the underlying legislation.  When a new cohort begins, the next version of the law “takes effect” for that group, while the previous cohort continues to be governed by the rules that were in place when they started.

---

### How it works in practice

| Step | What happens for the **2024‑25 cohort** | What happens for the **2025‑26 cohort** |
|------|------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| **1. Legislation is passed** | A law (or amendment) is enacted *before* the 2024‑25 intake. It sets tuition caps, funding formulas, eligibility criteria, residency rules, etc. | A new law (or amendment) is enacted *before* the 2025‑26 intake. It may change any of the same parameters. |
| **2. “Effective date”** | The legislation specifies that it becomes effective for students who **first enrol** in the 2024‑25 academic year. | The same legislation (or a later amendment) specifies that it becomes effective for students who **first enrol** in the 2025‑26 academic year. |
| **3. “Grand‑fathering” / transitional provisions** | The 2024‑25 cohort is *grand‑fathered* under the rules that were in force at the moment they started. Even if the law is amended in 2025, those changes do **not** retroactively alter their tuition rates, funding eligibility, or other rights, unless the legislation explicitly says otherwise. | The 2025‑26 cohort is subject to the new rules from day one. They cannot claim the older rates or eligibility criteria unless the new law includes a special transition clause. |
| **4. Ongoing study** | Throughout the length of their programme (e.g., 3‑ or 4‑year degree), the 2024‑25 cohort continues to be governed by the 2024‑25 version of the regulations, even if the university later adopts a different policy for new entrants. | The 2025‑26 cohort follows the 2025‑26 version of the regulations for the whole of their study period. |
| **5. Institutional implementation** | The university publishes a “cohort‑specific policy” (often in the student handbook or on its website) that references the 2024‑25 legislation. | The university publishes a separate policy document that references the 2025‑26 legislation. |

---

### Typical areas that differ between cohorts

| Policy area | What might change from one cohort to the next? |
|-------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| **Tuition fee caps** | Government may raise or lower the maximum amount a university can charge for a given programme. |
| **Student‑financial‑aid formulas** | Funding per credit hour, eligibility thresholds, or the mix of grants vs. loans can be revised. |
| **Residency / domicile rules** | The length of time a student must have lived in a province/state to qualify for “in‑province” tuition may be altered. |
| **Program‑duration requirements** | Minimum credit loads per term, maximum time to complete a degree, or mandatory co‑op placements can be updated. |
| **Admission standards** | Minimum GPA, language‑proficiency scores, or prerequisite courses may be tightened or relaxed. |
| **Leave‑of‑absence and re‑entry rules** | The number of permitted leaves, the process for returning, or the impact on funding may be changed. |
| **Graduation requirements** | New capstone projects, internship hours, or competency assessments can be introduced for a new cohort. |

---

### Why the distinction matters to students

1. **Financial planning** – A student who entered in 2024‑25 can rely on the tuition rates and financial‑aid rules that were announced at the time of admission; they won’t be hit with a sudden increase if the government raises caps for the 2025‑26 cohort.

2. **Eligibility for scholarships or bursaries** – Many awards are tied to the legislation that defines who qualifies (e.g., “students who began their studies in 2024‑25 or later”). A change in the law can open or close eligibility for a particular cohort.

3. **Program expectations** – If a new cohort is required to complete a mandatory internship that did not exist for the previous cohort, the university will apply that requirement only to the later entrants.

4. **Legal certainty** – Cohort legislation reduces the risk of retroactive policy changes, which could otherwise create disputes over contracts between the student and the institution.

---

### How you can find out which rules apply to you

1. **Check the university’s student handbook or policy portal.**  Most institutions label the relevant version (e.g., “Policy 2024‑25 – Tuition Fees”).  
2. **Look for the legislative citation.**  The document will often reference the specific act or regulation (e.g., “Provincial Higher‑Education Act, s. 12(1), effective for students first enrolled in 2024‑25”).  
3. **Contact the registrar or student‑services office.**  They can confirm which cohort‑specific regulations govern your programme.  
4. **Review any “transition” notices.**  When a new law is passed, universities usually publish a brief explaining which cohorts are affected and whether any special provisions apply.

---

### Bottom line

- **Cohort legislation ties a set of rules to the year a student first enrols.**  
- **Students entering in 2024‑25 are governed by the version of the law that took effect for that intake.**  
- **Students entering in 2025‑26 are governed by the next version, which may contain different tuition caps, funding formulas, residency requirements, and other programme rules.**  
- **Because the rules are “grand‑fathered,” changes made after a cohort’s start date generally do not affect those students, providing stability and predictability for each group.**  

If you need the exact wording of the legislation for your jurisdiction, you’ll need to consult the official government publication or your university’s policy documents; the general principles above apply in most systems that use cohort‑based regulation.

5. **Where to find the exact process for a given university** –

### 1. What you can safely use the tool for
| Task | How AI can help | What you must still do yourself |

- **Submitting AI‑generated text as your own work.** The essay should be written by you; the model can only act as a *coach* or *editor*.

3. **Review the AI’s feedback** critically; keep only the suggestions that improve clarity, logic, or flow.
4. **Revise the draft** manually, integrating the accepted suggestions and adding any missing evidence or citations.
5. **Run a final check** (spell‑check, plagiarism detector, citation manager) to ensure the paper meets all formal requirements.

- **Maintain your voice.** The goal is to sharpen your own reasoning, not to replace it.
- **Respect intellectual property.** If the AI suggests a quotation or a source you didn’t originally use, verify that you have the right to cite it and that it fits your argument.
- **Avoid over‑reliance.** Using the tool repeatedly for the same essay can hinder your own development of critical‑thinking and writing skills.

| Step | What the invigilator does | Why it matters |
|------|---------------------------|----------------|

| **2. Documentation** | The invigilator records the incident on the official *exam incident report* (or equivalent form). | **3. Reporting** | The incident is forwarded to the exam officer / faculty exam board or the university’s Academic Integrity Office. | **6. Support & appeal** | If the student believes the finding is unjust (e.g., the writing was a medical note, a therapeutic reminder, or an accidental smudge), they can usually appeal the decision through the university’s appeal mechanisms, often with supporting evidence (e.g., a doctor’s note). | Appeals protect the student’s right to a fair hearing and can overturn or reduce the sanction if the evidence warrants it. |

### Why writing on the skin is considered a breach

1. **Unauthorised aid** – Most exam regulations explicitly forbid any “concealed or hidden notes,” and the skin is treated the same as a piece of paper or a hidden device.
2. **Difficulty of monitoring** – Unlike a notebook, notes on the body are hard for invigilators to see, making them a preferred method for cheating if not caught.
3. **Equity** – Allowing one student to use such notes would give them an unfair advantage over peers who are abiding by the rules.

| Action | Rationale |
|--------|-----------|
